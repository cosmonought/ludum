Why 1830 is the first game

The 18xx that made the family, and why a market of shared companies is the right place for Ludum to start.

Published
8 Oct 2026

Ludum

All notes

Most 18xx games still start from 1830’s rules. Francis Tresham’s game, published by Avalon Hill in 1986, put railroads and a stock market on the same table, and made its players investors before it made them builders.1

Note 1The president holds the most shares, runs the company for every shareholder, and is the first to answer when it cannot pay.

That is the reason it comes first at Ludum. The market of shared companies — where you can own part of a railroad you do not run, and run one you barely own — is the idea every later game here borrows, 20 Cosmos included.

A game about capital that wears out

Trains rust. The day a newer train is bought, older ones leave the board, and a company that was rich on paper discovers it has to buy again. It is the rule players remember, and the one that makes 1830 a game about time as much as money.

A round of 1830 has three movements:

  1. Stock rounds, where players buy and sell shares.
  2. Operating rounds, where each company builds, runs its trains, and pays out or withholds.
  3. Phase changes, when the first of a new train is bought and the board’s rules move on.

What the Juno Edition keeps

The rules of the original, and the table talk. What changes is where the game is kept: the Juno Edition is being built for play on Juno, and its development is logged here as it happens.

What it doesn’t promise yet

Release dates, prices, player counts online: none of these are set, so none of them are on this page. When they are, they will be in a devlog first.
